Part one

What we are building, and why it matters

Written for everybody: teammates on any pod, lecturers, and the people in Ojota, Ogudu and Bariga this is meant to serve. No technical background assumed.

Nigeria does not have a rainfall problem. It has a warning problem.

Water that is already in your street is not news to you. The person who needs the message is the one two neighbourhoods downstream, who still has forty minutes and does not know it.

Flooding is the most destructive recurring natural hazard in Nigeria, and the numbers are not small or historical. They are annual.

662

People killed by flooding across Nigeria in a single year, 2022. Another 3,174 were injured.

2022 was not an outlier. In 2012 the floods killed 363 people, displaced 2.1 million and affected roughly seven million, with total losses assessed at US$16.9 billion and a 1.4 percent hit to real GDP growth. Flooding has returned every year since, and every year the same pattern repeats: the water arrives before the warning does.

Warning early is the single cheapest thing that works

This is the part that makes the project worth building rather than merely worth writing about. Early warning is not a marginal improvement on disaster response. It is the intervention with the best evidence behind it.

EkoAlert does not need new sensors, new masts or new infrastructure. It needs the thing Lagos already has in abundance: people with phones who can see the water.

Why Lagos, and why this corridor

Lagos is close to a worst case. Roughly 21 to 25 million people live in a city whose average elevation is one to four metres above sea level, on ground that is subsiding by up to 87 millimetres a year, drained by channels that silt up, fill with refuse and get built over.

The share of the city classified as flood-vulnerable rose from about 1 percent in 2000 to nearly 35 percent by 2019. Lagos ranks 15th out of 136 global port cities for population exposure to coastal flooding under a 2070s climate scenario.

The water is predictable. It runs downhill, along channels, in a sequence people who live there already know by heart.

Our pilot corridor runs Ojota through Ogudu to Bariga, following the Drainage Channel, Ogudu Creek and Agboyi Creek. We did not choose it arbitrarily. It floods, on the record, on a schedule:

  • In July 2024, heavy rain flooded Ketu-Alapere and Oshodi-Mafoluku among others. A student was swept away by floodwater in the Ketu area.
  • In 2025, floodwater took over the bridge linking Ojota to Maryland along Ikorodu Road, stranding vehicles and commuters.
  • Reporting on the same corridor notes that the drainage channel which drains Bariga and communities downstream has been taken over by squatters and by property owners who built across it, obstructing the flow.

That last point is not background colour. It is a requirement: any model of this corridor has to be able to represent a channel that used to work and no longer does. Our system carries a blocked flag on every connection for exactly this reason, and any reporter can raise it with one tap.

The idea in one page

Lagos already has flood communication. WhatsApp groups and TikTok spread news of flooding faster and further than this project ever will. We are not competing with them, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

But those channels have three properties that make them insufficient, and each one is a design requirement for us.

What broadcast channels doWhy it falls shortWhat EkoAlert does instead
Describe water that is already there A video of flooding in Ojota tells the man two zones downstream nothing, and he is the person who can still act Predicts forward along the drainage, with a rough time to impact
Address everybody at once People learn that most messages are not about them, and stop reading Warns only the zones water is actually expected to reach, to people subscribed to those zones
Carry no verification Old flood footage recirculates every season and is believed again Requires two independent vetted reporters in the same zone, inside a time window, before anything escalates

How it works, for a resident

  1. Someone reports waterA vetted community reporter in Ojota opens the app and taps one of three depths. It takes about three seconds.
  2. A second person agreesNothing happens on one report. When a second, different reporter in the same zone logs water inside the time window, the zone escalates. One person tapping twice never counts.
  3. The system looks downstreamIt walks a map of how water actually moves through this corridor, zone by zone, and works out which places are next and roughly how long they have.
  4. Only the affected are warnedPeople subscribed to those downstream zones get a message naming their zone, the expected depth, and the minutes until it arrives.
  5. Residents correct the mapEvery connection on the map can be confirmed or rejected from the map itself, in one tap. Residents can also name places the map does not have at all.

The three depths

We deliberately avoided centimetres. Nobody standing in a flooded street measures anything. People describe water by what it stops them doing, so that is what we ask.

Ankle
Passable on foot. Shoes are ruined, the day continues.
Knee
Cars begin to stall. Small vehicles should not attempt it.
Impassable
Do not enter on foot or by vehicle. Move to higher ground.

The Sustainable Development Goal this serves

EkoAlert targets SDG 11: Sustainable Cities and Communities, and specifically Target 11.5:

“By 2030, significantly reduce the number of deaths and the number of people affected and substantially decrease the direct economic losses relative to global gross domestic product caused by disasters, including water-related disasters, with a focus on protecting the poor and people in vulnerable situations.”

It also serves SDG 13: Climate Action, Target 13.1, on strengthening resilience and adaptive capacity to climate-related hazards.

Both targets are measured by the same indicator, 11.5.1 and 13.1.1: the number of deaths, missing persons and directly affected persons attributed to disasters per 100,000 population.

That matters for how we judge our own project. The indicator counts people affected, not only people killed. Someone evacuated in time, whose livelihood survives because they moved a generator or a stall an hour earlier, is a number that moves. A warning system aims squarely at that indicator, and the phrase “with a focus on protecting the poor and people in vulnerable situations” describes the informal, low-lying, poorly drained settlements along our pilot corridor almost exactly.

What this cannot do, stated plainly

A project defence is stronger for naming its weaknesses first. These are ours.

Reach

On day one EkoAlert has no users. A WhatsApp group with four hundred members beats us instantly and will keep beating us. Our answer is not to compete: informal channels are our distribution route. A zone chairman pasting an EkoAlert warning into his estate group is the system working exactly as intended.

The map starts out mostly guesswork

We seeded the drainage map by geometric inference so it would be complete on day one, but an inferred connection is a guess and we treat it as one. No guessed connection is allowed to send a warning. So at launch the system shows a full map and sends almost nothing, and gets progressively louder as residents confirm connections. That transition is the product.

It cannot tell you the water is coming if nobody reports it

The system is only as awake as its reporters. This is a genuine dependency and the reason reporter vetting and retention matter as much as any code we write.

Predictions compound error

Each hop downstream multiplies uncertainty, so we cap propagation at two to three hops. A false alarm destroys trust far faster than a missed alarm does, and a system nobody believes warns nobody.

Part two

How we solved it

For the Engine, Platform, Frontend and Delivery pods, and for anyone marking the design. Diagrams, data model, algorithms and the reasoning behind the constraints.

Context diagram

The context diagram fixes the system boundary: EkoAlert as a single process, the four external entities it exchanges data with, and nothing about its internals.

Note the direction of the OpenStreetMap flow. Geography enters the system once, at seeding, and never again. Everything that follows comes from people.

Data flow diagram

Decomposing process 0 gives the five processes that do the work, and the six data stores they read and write. The spine runs left to right: a report arrives, it is tested for quorum, an escalation propagates downstream, and warnings are delivered.

Two things sit off the spine deliberately. The administrator gates delivery through the kill switch, which is a control flow rather than a data flow. And process 5.0 runs on a completely different clock from the rest: corrections trickle in over weeks and slowly improve the graph that process 3.0 depends on.

The graph model, and the claim an edge actually makes

This is the conceptual core of the project, and the thing most likely to be misunderstood.

Zones are nodes: a street cluster, an estate, a ward. Connections between them are directed edges. But an edge from A to B does not claim that a physical channel runs between them. It claims something weaker and far more useful:

When water is reported in A, water tends to appear in B roughly N minutes later.

That is an observational claim sourced from residents, not a hydrological one. The distinction drives every design decision that follows, because it means an edge can be true without anyone understanding why it is true, and it means the people best placed to supply it are the ones who have watched it happen every rainy season.

Why we could not just compute this

The obvious approach is to derive flow direction from elevation data. It does not work here, and the reason is worth stating precisely: Lagos is too flat. At SRTM resolution the height differences that actually route water through the city are smaller than the sensor's own error. Standard D8 flow-direction analysis on that data produces confident nonsense.

Nor could we lift the topology from OpenStreetMap. We pulled 95 km of waterway geometry for the pilot corridor and got 105 segments in 44 disconnected components. That fragmentation is not a setback to work around, it is the evidence for the whole project: the drainage topology of this corridor is not in any dataset, and it has to come from the people who live on it.

So the seed graph is built by a deterministic geometric rule, reproducible and checkable by anyone: the next zone downstream along the corridor line, with direction taken as toward the lagoon and travel time estimated at a provisional 55 metres per minute. It is honest scaffolding, and it is explicitly labelled as a guess in the database.

The graph lives in the database with an admin editing interface. It is never hardcoded, because it will be wrong and it must be fixable without a redeploy. Traversal tolerates cycles, because Lagos drainage has them.

The confidence rule

This is the single constraint that makes the system trustworthy, and it is worth stating as a rule rather than a feature:

Every edge is seeded by inference so the map is complete on day one. No inferred edge may ever fire an alert.

So the system launches showing a full, honest map of guesses that sends almost nothing. An edge becomes confirmed only when enough separate residents affirm it, or when observed reports show the timing holds. As edges cross that threshold the system gets progressively louder.

Correcting the map is one tap, not a form

A form gets used by nobody. Every connection on the map can be confirmed or rejected directly from the zone it belongs to, and the vote count is visible so people can see how close an edge is to flipping.

Three rules govern the tally, and each one came from an actual failure we hit while testing:

  • The threshold counts people, not taps. One person tapping confirm twice never flips anything.
  • Only a person's most recent position counts. Tapping yes and then no leaves you as one rejection, not one of each. Every tap is still written to the log, because that log is the evidence trail. It is the tally that reads the latest stance, not the history that forgets.
  • Nothing is permanent. Two separate residents can flip a confirmed edge to rejected, and two can flip it back. A rejected edge is not deleted, because a rejection is evidence about the terrain rather than a mistake to erase.

Residents can add nodes, not just edges

Correcting an edge assumes both of its ends already exist. Frequently neither does. The pilot stops at 20 seeded zones and water does not, so the honest answer to “where does it go next” is often a place the graph has never heard of.

A resident can name that place. Two things about it go missing separately, and the design treats them separately:

Required?Reasoning
LandmarkYesA place nobody can name is a place nobody else can corroborate
PositionNoThe person who knows what somewhere is called is not always the person standing at it. A later voice can supply the pin

A proposed place waits in its own table rather than landing in the zone table directly, because the zone table is referenced by alerts, subscriptions and reporters: an unvetted row there would be instantly subscribable and alertable. Promotion needs a name, a position, and the same threshold of distinct voices. The edge it arrives with is inferred, so naming a place warns nobody until that edge is separately confirmed.

A design lesson worth keeping

Our first version located places using the phone's GPS alone. A tester tried to name a street he could see on the map near his zone, and the pin landed 12.5 kilometres away, producing an edge with a 228-minute travel time.

The bug was conceptual, not technical. GPS answers “where am I”, which is only the same question as “where is the place” when the person is standing in it. Someone recognising a street two zones away on the map is doing exactly what inference cannot do, and we had given them no way to say it. The fix was a crosshair they drag over the map, with the distance from the origin zone shown live, plus server-side limits that refuse a position on top of the origin or beyond 5 km.

Quorum, escalation and the propagation engine

Quorum

A zone escalates when two different vetted reporters log water in it within a 45-minute window. The window matters: two reports six hours apart describe two events, not one. The count is of distinct people, so a single reporter cannot escalate a zone alone no matter how many times they tap. Suspended and unverified reporters have their reports stored but not counted, which keeps the record complete without letting it drive alerts.

Propagation

Once a zone escalates, the engine walks the drainage graph downstream from it, best-first, accumulating travel time along each path. It emits a predicted alert for each zone it reaches, carrying the severity, the accumulated minutes and the hop count.

Four constraints shape the traversal:

  • Depth is capped at two to three hops. Errors compound and false alarms destroy trust faster than missed alarms do.
  • Cycles are tolerated. Lagos drainage genuinely contains them, so the traversal tracks visited zones rather than assuming a tree.
  • Weak edges propagate at reduced severity or not at all. Confidence is an input to the traversal, not a filter applied afterwards.
  • Blocked edges are downweighted or disabled until reported clear.

Why the engine is pure Java with no Spring

The propagation engine has no framework dependencies at all. It is pure functions over records, which buys three things: it is unit-testable in isolation with nothing to mock and no database, it is reusable for any city given a graph, and its behaviour is pinned by an immutable golden scenario. If a golden case fails, the engine is wrong. We never edit an assertion to make a test pass.

De-escalation

A zone with no new report for 90 minutes clears, and an all-clear goes out. Telling people it is over is part of the job; a warning system that only ever escalates teaches people to ignore it.

The kill switch

An administrator can halt every outgoing alert immediately. This is a first-class feature that shipped early, not a nice-to-have. Any system that pushes warnings to real people needs an off button, and ours is deliberately placed so that halting delivery does not stop the system recording reports or escalating zones: the evidence trail survives the intervention.

Replay mode

The system can replay a past flood event against the current graph: feed timestamped historical reports in, watch what the engine would have predicted, and compare against what actually happened. This is both our primary validation mechanism for the graph and the centrepiece of the project defence. It is how we answer “does this actually work” with something other than an opinion.

Testing posture

The suite currently stands at 133 backend tests passing, across three layers: pure engine tests over records with no infrastructure, domain service tests, and integration tests running against a real PostGIS database with the real migrations via Testcontainers.

Two rules override convenience. Engine tests are written before the code they cover. And the golden scenario is immutable.

Data provenance, the hard boundary

Every column in the seed data is tagged with where it came from and whether it can be trusted. Coordinates and corridor names come from OpenStreetMap and are reliable. Travel times and downstream links are geometric inference and are provisional. Zone names and landmarks come from a field survey that has not happened, and are never populated by inference, which is why the interface falls back to a zone id rather than inventing a name. Four zones sit more than 2 km from any named place on OpenStreetMap: they are not empty land, they need a person.

Junction edges between the three corridors were deliberately left blank. Those are the edges that matter most and the ones inference is worst at, so they are exactly the edges we want residents to supply rather than guess at ourselves.

About these figures

Casualty figures for large floods vary between sources and between reporting dates, because counts are revised as assessments complete. The 2022 Nigeria death toll appears as “over 600”, 603 and 662 in different reports; we use NEMA's own end-of-year figure of 662 and note the range here rather than picking silently. Lagos population is given as a range for the same reason: metropolitan and state boundaries are counted differently.

Before submission, verify each figure against its primary source. Secondary reporting drifts, and a marker is entitled to check.
